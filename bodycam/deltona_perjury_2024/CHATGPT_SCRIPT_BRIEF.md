## 1. Case summary (one paragraph)

On **May 16, 2024, ~10:17 p.m.**, a Volusia Sheriff's Office (VSO) deputy stopped a pickup truck near
**E. Normandy Blvd / Providence Blvd, Deltona, Florida** because of a tail light that wasn't working. The driver
would not hand over his license, registration or insurance and asked for a supervisor. A sergeant came, said who he
was, and asked for the documents again. The driver still didn't hand them over, so he was taken out of the truck,
handcuffed, searched and taken to jail. **The next day (May 17, 2024)** the driver went to the VSO Deltona substation
and filed a **sworn** Citizen's Complaint. He said deputies **slammed him to the ground, slammed him against his
truck, choked him**, and that the sergeant said "get the f\*\*\* in the car before I kick you in the car." VSO's
internal inquiry reviewed body camera footage from the deputies on scene. The Sheriff says it shows the allegations
were false. On **June 5, 2024** deputies arrested the driver at his home on a warrant for **perjury** (a felony), with
bond set at **$50,000**. Sheriff Mike Chitwood released the edited footage publicly on **June 6, 2024**.

**Why it works as a documentary:** it's a self-contained "claim vs. camera" story. The man's sworn words can be
checked line by line against several body cameras. The twist is that his own complaint got him arrested a second time.
It's non-graphic: no weapons, no injuries shown, no violence beyond a handcuffing.

Legal context you can explain (general law, not a claim about the outcome):
- **Florida Statute 837.02**: perjury in an official proceeding is a third-degree felony.
  **F.S. 837.012**: perjury when not in an official proceeding is a first-degree misdemeanor. Which subsection VSO used
  is **UNVERIFIED**. Say "a felony perjury charge, according to the Sheriff's Office."
- **F.S. 316.221 / 316.610**: tail lamps must work; an equipment violation is a lawful basis for a stop.
- **F.S. 322.15**: drivers must show their license when a law enforcement officer asks.
- The VSO citizen complaint is a **sworn** form (the Sheriff calls it a "sworn affidavit"). A knowingly false
  statement under oath can be prosecuted as perjury. Complaints against officers are handled under **F.S. 112.533**.
  Don't add more legal detail than this unless it has been checked.
- Florida's Law Enforcement Officers' Bill of Rights covers how officers are investigated. A citizen complaint triggers
  an internal inquiry (here: Lt. Bello).

## 14. Explanation opportunities

1. Can police stop you for a tail light? (Yes. Equipment violation, F.S. 316.221.)
2. Do you have to show ID to a deputy? (Drivers must show a license, F.S. 322.15. This is different from passengers
   or pedestrians.)
3. Asking for a supervisor: allowed, but it doesn't pause the stop.
4. What "perjury" means and why a complaint form is sworn.
5. Why bodycams protect **both** sides. The video that cleared the deputies also caught an officer swearing (3:49).
6. Agency-released edits vs. raw footage: what viewers should keep in mind.
7. Arrest ≠ conviction. The presumption of innocence.
